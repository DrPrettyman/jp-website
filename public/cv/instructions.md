# Cover-letter writing instructions:

## Style Guide 1: avoid "written-by-AI giveaways"

### Critical Writing Rules

- **No dramatic colons** — never use "X: Y, Z, and W" to list things after a colon for effect. Write "Wave works with millions of users and sparse data, which requires..." not "Wave's problem space: millions of users, sparse data, and complex tradeoffs".
- **No rule of three** — never list exactly three parallel items for rhetorical effect. Use two items, four items, or just one. Two is usually enough.
- **Don't describe the job back** — the reader wrote the job description. Don't list what the job requires or what the company does. Show how your experience connects. Write "At Blink I built an ML pipeline processing 50M daily records, which taught me..." not "The role requires ML experience and working with large datasets".
- **No formulaic structure** — don't start every paragraph with "I". Don't make paragraphs the same length. A two-sentence paragraph followed by a longer one feels human.
- **No empty connectors** — cut "which is why", "this is what drew me to", "that's exactly what", "this is where". State the connection directly or let the reader infer it.

### AI Writing Guidelines

- **No contrastive reframes** — never use "It wasn't just X, it was Y" or "This isn't about X, it's about Y". Just state what it is directly.
- **No negation for false depth** — avoid "more than just", "not only... but also", "not simply about". Make the point without the negation setup.
- **No rule of three** — don't use three parallel items for rhetorical effect ("expertise, passion, and commitment"). Use two items or four, or just one.
- **No paragraph-opening hedges** — never start with "When it comes to...", "In today's rapidly evolving...", "In the realm of...". Start with the actual subject.
- **No flattering intensifiers** — avoid "fascinating", "captivating", "remarkable", "compelling", "truly", "deeply", "genuinely". Don't call anything a "journey" or "transformation". Let facts speak.
- **No excessive transitions** — don't use "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Indeed", "In summary". Don't start multiple sentences with "This" referring back. If ideas connect, the connection should be obvious.
- **Vary paragraph length and structure** — don't make every paragraph the same length or follow the same pattern. A two-sentence paragraph followed by a longer one feels human.
- **No em-dashes** — use colons, commas, or periods instead of em-dashes (--).
- **No generic phrases** — never use "I am writing to apply", "aligns closely with", "I would welcome the opportunity", "I am excited to".
- **No hook lines with dramatic colons** — don't use "The problem was simple:" or "Here's the thing:" to create artificial tension. Write "The problem was that..." or just state the point directly.
- **No LinkedIn buzzwords** — never use "at the intersection of", "in the X space", "leverage", "at scale", "ecosystem", "synergy", "thought leadership". These are content-marketing language, not human speech.
- **No empty intensifiers for experience** — don't say experience "runs deep", skills are "extensive", or background is "strong". Either give specifics or just state the fact plainly ("I've built APIs" not "My API experience runs deep").
- **No generic closings** — don't end with vague enthusiasm like "I'd like to help build whatever comes next" or "exactly where I want to be". End with something specific or just stop after your last substantive point.
- **No performative tone** — write as if speaking to one person, not posting content for an audience. If a sentence would work as a LinkedIn post, rewrite it.
- **No indirect phrasing** — don't say "X is something I care about" or "Y is something I've always valued". Just state it directly: "I care about X" or better, show it through what you've done.
- **Vary paragraph starts** — don't start every paragraph with "I" or follow the same subject-verb pattern. Start with the work, the company, a specific detail, or a short sentence that isn't "I [verb]".

## Style Guide 2: Cover letter tips

### Structure and length
- Keep it concise (250-350 words).
- Focus on 2-3 strong connections, not every topic.
- Don't repeat your resume. The cover letter should add context and narrative, not duplicate bullet points.
- Vary sentence and paragraph length. Not every paragraph should start with 'I'.

### Opening
- The opening sentence must hook immediately. No "I was excited to see your posting..." or "I am writing to apply for...".
- Lead with a specific company challenge or need, then pivot to how your experience addresses it. Don't flatter the company generically, but showing you understand their problem is compelling.

### Content
- Be specific: include metrics and concrete details.
- Frame around the value you deliver, not what you want. "I'm looking for a role where I can grow" is a red flag.
- Lead with INDUSTRY experience. Academic background supports but doesn't lead, unless the research is directly relevant to the role.
- Do not list academic publications. GOOD: "During my PhD I researched novel methods for detecting..." BAD: "During my PhD I researched novel methods for detecting... which was published in Journal Of Science and Science Letters"
- Customize each letter for the specific role. Reference the company, role, and recent news or challenges. Don't just swap out the company name.

### Tone
- Sound natural and friendly, like you're talking to a colleague. Not corporate, not salesy, not like a LinkedIn post.
- Use contractions (I'm, I've, wasn't). Match the company's tone for more conservative environments.
- Write to one person, not to an audience. Address a specific person by name when possible (check LinkedIn or the company website).

### Closing
- End with something specific or just stop after your last substantive point. No vague enthusiasm.

### Before sending
- Proofread meticulously. A single spelling or grammar error can disqualify you.
